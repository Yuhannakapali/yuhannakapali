# The Trash Husband's Multi-Billion Empire

### Book One: The Man in the Mirror

*A note before you begin: this story opens on the aftermath of domestic violence. The abuse belongs to the man who used to live in this body, and the novel is about the woman and child surviving it, not about excusing it. Shu Mang's recovery moves at her pace, never at her husband's convenience.*

---

## Chapter One: The Wrong Body

The last thing Chen Zuang remembered was the smell of good leather.

He had been in the back of the Maybach on Century Avenue, forty minutes out from the signing that would have folded Hengtai Logistics into his group and made him, at fifty-one, the largest private freight holder south of the Yangtze. He remembered reaching for the folder. He remembered a heat in his left arm like a wire pulled tight. He remembered his secretary saying his name twice, the second time in a register he had never heard from her, and then nothing at all.

The next thing he smelled was piss.

Not entirely his own. Old beer soaked into a mattress, cigarette ash gone damp, something sweet and rotten in a corner where a bag had split. He opened his eyes onto a water-stained ceiling nine feet away and a fluorescent tube with one dead end, buzzing like an insect trapped in glass.

He tried to sit up. The body obeyed the way a badly maintained machine obeys: everything moved, nothing moved well. His hands came into view and he stopped breathing.

They were young hands. Twenty-something hands, knuckles split and scabbed, nicotine yellow between the first two fingers of the right, a cheap steel ring on the thumb. Not his. His own hands had been soft, manicured, spotted at the wrist with age. These belonged to a stranger, and they were attached to him.

He got himself off the mattress on the third attempt and found a mirror above a sink full of grey water. The face in it was not quite thirty and looked forty. Sunken eyes, three days of beard, a scab on the cheekbone, hair like something scraped off a drain. Handsome underneath it all, in the way of men who have thrown a good face away with both hands.

Chen Zuang, chairman of Zhaoyuan Capital Holdings, looked at that face for a long time.

Then he noticed the silence.

It was not the silence of an empty apartment. It was the other kind, the kind he had heard exactly once in his life, in a warehouse in Wenzhou when a foreman had gone in swinging a length of rebar at a man who owed him money and everyone else in the building had gone quiet at the same moment, the way animals do.

He turned around.

The main room of the apartment was perhaps twenty square meters. There was a folding table with one leg wedged on a brick, a rice cooker, a wall calendar from a fertilizer company two years out of date. In the far corner, between the wardrobe and the window, a woman was sitting on the floor with her arms around a child.

She was thin in the way that is not fashion. Hair pulled back with a rubber band, a work shirt with a laundry's name embroidered over the pocket, and she was watching him with an attention so total that it took him a moment to understand it was not attention at all. It was calculation. She was measuring the distance from her body to the door, and from the door to him, and she had already done it several times.

There was a bruise on her jaw, four days old by the color, greening at the edge.

The child in her lap could not have been five. A girl. She had her face pressed into her mother's collarbone and she was not making any sound, and that was the thing that undid him, because a child of five who is frightened should be screaming, and a child of five who has learned not to scream has been taught.

"You're up," the woman said.

Her voice was flat and pleasant and utterly empty, and he understood that she was speaking that way on purpose, that this was a technique she had developed, that somewhere in the last few years she had learned that a certain tone bought her thirty seconds.

"There's congee," she said. "I'll heat it. Nuonuo will be quiet, she's just tired, she won't make noise."

*She won't make noise.* An offering. A price paid in advance.

Chen Zuang had negotiated against men who could have bought a small province. He had sat across from a state bank vice president for eleven hours without conceding a percentage point. He had, in his life, been called cold by people who meant it as a compliment, and he had earned every syllable of it.

He put a hand on the wall, turned into the sink, and was sick until nothing else came.

When it stopped he stayed bent over, gripping the porcelain, and let his mind do what it always did, which was work.

*Facts. Establish the facts.*

He was not dreaming. Dreams do not include the taste of another man's mouth. He was in a body that was not his, in a room that was not his, and there was a woman in the corner who flinched when he moved his shoulder.

The body knew things. When he reached for them they came up like objects from muddy water, slow and in pieces. A name: also Chen Zuang. He almost laughed, and the laugh turned into something else in his throat. Heaven had a sense of humor and it was a cheap one. Twenty-nine years old. A wife, Shu Mang, twenty-seven. A daughter, Chen Nuo, called Nuonuo, four years and eight months.

And then the rest of it came up out of the water, and he wished very much that it had not.

The mahjong parlor behind the wet market. The loans. The thing this body had done last Thursday when Shu Mang had hidden the rent money in the rice, and the sound the folding table had made against the wall.

He was still holding the sink. His new hands were shaking.

*You are in the house of a man who beat this woman,* he thought, *and to her, you are him.*

He straightened up slowly. He did not turn all the way around. He kept his hands where she could see them.

"Shu Mang," he said, and his voice came out wrong, hoarse and low. He tried again. "I'm not going to touch you."

Nothing. The flat pleasant nothing of her face.

"I'm going to sit down over here," he said, "by the door, which is behind me, so you and Nuonuo are between the window and the wardrobe and nothing is between you and the hallway except me, and I am going to sit on the floor. All right?"

He sat. It put his eyes below hers. He had used this once in a room full of union representatives and it had felt like strategy. It did not feel like strategy now.

Shu Mang did not move. But the child, very slightly, turned her head, and one eye looked out at him from her mother's shirt.

"Say what you want," Shu Mang said. "It's easier if you just say it."

"I don't want anything."

"You always want something."

"He did," Chen Zuang said. "I don't."

It was the wrong thing. He watched her face close like a shop shutter and understood that he had just said something that sounded, to her, like the beginning of a performance she had sat through before. The apology. The tears. The promise. The week of good behavior and then the Thursday.

So he stopped talking. He sat on the floor by the door of a stranger's apartment in a body he had not earned, and he waited, and he did the only useful thing available to a man in his position, which was to begin, very carefully, to count what he had.

He had no money, no name, no credit, no reputation except a rotten one.

He had thirty years of knowing exactly how money moves through a room.

And he had, he thought, looking at the woman in the corner who would not stop watching his hands, approximately the rest of his life to make this right, and it would not be nearly long enough.

## Chapter Two: The Inventory

He did not sleep that night. He took the floor by the door and lay on his back with his eyes open, listening to the woman in the corner not sleeping either.

At some point after two she got up, moved the child onto the mattress, and stood in the dark a moment. He kept his breathing slow and even. He heard the small metallic sound of something being taken out from under the wardrobe and put back, and he knew without seeing it that there was a bag packed under there, and that she checked it the way other people check a lock.

Good, he thought. Keep it. Keep it where you can reach it.

At five thirty she left for the laundry. She took Nuonuo with her, because there was no one to leave her with, and the child walked out the door in a coat too thin for October without once looking at him.

The moment the stairwell went quiet, he started the audit.

He had done this a hundred times to companies. You do not begin with the story a business tells about itself. You begin with the drawers.

Under the mattress: nothing. Behind the rice cooker: a cigarette pack with two bent cigarettes and forty-three yuan in notes. In the wardrobe, in a shoebox, in a plastic sleeve, the real accounting.

Pawn tickets, eleven of them. A gold bracelet. A jade pendant with a woman's name on the back. A washing machine. A phone, twice, the same phone, redeemed and re-pawned.

Loan documents, four. Three from the same outfit, Yongfa Consulting, which was a mahjong parlor with a business license. Handwritten interest terms in the margin, thumbprint in red at the bottom of each. Principal of one hundred and forty thousand yuan. Accrued, by the numbers written there in ballpoint, three hundred and eighty-two thousand.

An eviction notice from the landlord, second and final, dated eleven days ago.

A hospital receipt from the district clinic, seven months old. A four-year-old's wrist. The attending physician's note said *fall from bed.* He read it three times and then put it face down on the table because he could not look at it and think at the same time.

And at the bottom of the box, folded into a small hard square, a certificate. Shu Mang, first place, provincial youth mathematics olympiad, aged sixteen. Under it, an acceptance letter to a university accounting program she had never attended, dated the year Nuonuo was born.

He sat with that one for a while too.

By noon he had the balance sheet, and it was the worst he had ever seen. Liabilities: three hundred and eighty-two thousand and a beating a week. Assets: forty-three yuan, an expired ID card, two hands that shook in the mornings, and a brain that had once turned a bankrupt shipping line into eleven billion.

He also had one thing more, and it took him until the afternoon to be sure of it.

The calendar on the wall said the year. It was a year he remembered. In his own life, in his own body, he had spent that autumn in Shenzhen watching a company called Ruiheng Materials climb on the strength of a lithium contract that did not exist, and he remembered, because he had made ninety million on it, exactly which week in December the auditors had walked.

So. Not only skill. A little foreknowledge, thin and fading at the edges like a dream, but real.

Chen Zuang put the shoebox back exactly as he had found it, because it was hers, and went out to look at the city.

Jiangcheng in the autumn, the old district: a wet market, a lane of hardware shops, three floors of scaffolding on every second building. He walked it for four hours with forty-three yuan in his pocket, and he did not look at it the way a poor man looks at a city. He looked at it the way he had looked at Wenzhou in 1998, which is to say he looked for the seams.

He found one at the end of Fuxing Lane.

A machining workshop, sixty square meters, four lathes, the shutter half down at two in the afternoon on a Tuesday. Through the gap he could see an old man sitting on an oil drum with a stack of paper in his hands, not reading it, just holding it.

Chen Zuang had built his first fortune on exactly this: a man sitting still in a building that should have been loud.

He ducked under the shutter.

"We're closed," the old man said.

"You're not closed," Chen Zuang said. "You're finished. Those are the acceptance criteria on a supply contract and you've just worked out that clause nine lets them reject the whole batch on a tolerance you can't hit with those lathes, and they'll do it, because they've already found a cheaper shop and they want their deposit back."

The old man looked up.

He had a face like a walnut and eyes that had been crying about an hour ago and had decided not to mention it. He looked Chen Zuang up and down: the scab, the stink, the shaking hands.

"Who the hell are you?"

"Nobody at all," Chen Zuang said, and pulled up a second oil drum. "Show me clause nine."

## Chapter Three: Clause Nine

The old man's name was Qi Debiao and he had run the shop for thirty-one years.

The contract was with Fanglin Precision, a mid-tier component maker that had grown fat enough to start eating its own suppliers. Eighty thousand parts, a deposit of ninety thousand yuan already spent on steel, delivery in nineteen days, and a tolerance specification of two hundredths of a millimeter that Qi Debiao's machines could hold on a good day with a following wind and could not hold across eighty thousand pieces.

"So I give the deposit back," Qi Debiao said, "and I sell the lathes, and I go live with my daughter in Wuxi and eat her husband's rice for the rest of my life."

"You don't have the deposit."

"No."

"Then that's not one of your options." Chen Zuang turned the page. "Read me clause four."

"I've read it forty times."

"Read it to me anyway."

Qi Debiao read it. Delivery in lots of no fewer than ten thousand units, inspection within seven days of receipt of each lot, payment on acceptance.

"Now clause nine."

"Rejection of any lot for tolerance failure permits cancellation of the whole agreement and return of the deposit within fifteen days."

"Cancellation of the whole agreement," Chen Zuang repeated. "Not of the lot. Of the agreement. Who drafted this?"

"Their office."

"Their office had a template and a junior. Look at the clause numbers, four and nine were written by two different people who never spoke to each other." He put his finger on the page. "Nine gives them the right to cancel. Four obliges them to inspect within seven days of receipt and pay on acceptance. It does not oblige them to inspect *before* you deliver the next lot. So."

He waited. He always waited. You learn more about a man from what he does in four seconds of silence than from an hour of his talking.

Qi Debiao's eyes moved along the page, and then they moved back, and then his mouth opened.

"I deliver everything at once," he said slowly.

"You deliver everything in eight lots over nine days, all before their first inspection window closes. Then there is no next lot to cancel. There is only eighty thousand parts sitting in their warehouse and a purchasing manager who has to explain to his director why the line is short if he sends them back."

"And if they reject them anyway?"

"Then they reject eighty thousand parts they have already stacked, unstacked, and had signed for by a gate clerk, and you have their signature on eight receipt notes, and the argument stops being about tolerance and starts being about paperwork. Which is a different argument, and it is one you can afford to lose slowly." Chen Zuang set the contract down. "You cannot afford to lose quickly, Uncle Qi. That's all this is. We are not making you win. We are making them take nine months to beat you, and in nine months you will have money."

The old man stared at him.

"You said you were nobody."

"I am, at the moment."

"What do you want?"

And here it was, the moment he had been walking toward all afternoon, and Chen Zuang felt the old cold clarity come down over him like a coat that fit.

"Three things," he said. "One. Nobody hears my name. Ever, in connection with this shop. If anyone asks, I sweep your floor. Two. When you get paid, I take twelve percent, and I take it as equity, not cash."

"Equity in *this*?" Qi Debiao gestured at the four lathes, the oil-stained floor, the buzzing light.

"In this. Twelve percent, written down, dated, chopped with your seal today."

"And three?"

Chen Zuang looked out through the gap in the shutter at the lane, where a woman in a laundry uniform was walking home with a child on her hip, forty minutes early, because the laundry had cut her hours again.

"Three," he said. "You have a spare room upstairs full of scrap. I want to use it in the evenings. And I want a job, on the books, at whatever you'd pay a bad apprentice, and I want you to pay me in cash on Fridays in an envelope with the shop's name on it."

Qi Debiao frowned. "You want less money and a worse job."

"I want a receipt," Chen Zuang said. "I want to be able to put something on a table at home and have it be provably ordinary." He stood, and put out one of those unfamiliar, shaking hands. "There is a woman in my house who has been lied to about money by this face for four years. The first true thing I put in front of her has to be small enough to believe."

## Chapter Four: Yongfa Comes to Collect

They came on Friday, which he had expected, because men like that always come on the day other men get paid.

Three of them, and the one who mattered was in the middle: soft, forty, a fake leather jacket, a phone in his hand held like a weapon. Bao Wu, and the body knew him the way a dog knows a stick.

Shu Mang was home. That was the part Chen Zuang had not been able to arrange. She was standing at the folding table with a knife in her hand and half an onion under it, and when the knock came her whole body went still in a way he had come to recognize and hate.

"Nuonuo," she said quietly. "Wardrobe."

The child went into the wardrobe. She did it fast and without being told twice, and she pulled the door most of the way shut behind her, and Chen Zuang thought: *she has a drill. My daughter has a drill.*

He opened the door before Bao Wu could kick it.

"Ah," Bao Wu said, and smiled with a lot of teeth. "He's alive. We heard you were dead."

"Not yet."

"Three eighty-two, Chen Zuang." He came in without being asked, and his two friends came in after him, and one of them looked at Shu Mang the way you look into a shop window. "Three eighty-two, and today's the day we agreed."

"Sit down," Chen Zuang said.

"What?"

"Sit down. That stool. Not that one, it's got a bad leg, that one." He pulled out the folding chair and set it in front of the table and waited, and Bao Wu, who had come here expecting a man on his knees or a man swinging, found himself sitting down out of sheer confusion.

Chen Zuang put a sheet of paper on the table between them.

"One hundred and forty thousand principal," he said. "Over twenty-two months. Your handwriting, your margin, your terms: fifteen percent per month, compounding, plus a service fee of two thousand each time you renew, which you have done eleven times." He turned the page around so it faced Bao Wu. "That's an annualized rate of four hundred and thirty-five percent. Would you like to guess what the judicial protection limit is?"

Bao Wu's smile stayed where it was, but it stopped being connected to anything.

"You think a court's going to help you?"

"I think a court is going to help *you*," Chen Zuang said. "That's what I want you to understand, because you're not stupid, you're just used to easy work. Nobody in this room can pay you three hundred and eighty-two thousand yuan. Not today, not in a year. If you break my legs, you get a man with broken legs who owes you three hundred and eighty-two thousand yuan. That's the whole menu.

"Now. The court will not enforce four thirty-five. The court will enforce the principal and something in the region of the protection ceiling, and it will do it against a signed instrument, and it will do it whether or not I like it, and there is a bailiff attached to the outcome. Which means the piece of paper in your hand is currently worth zero and could be worth two hundred and something thousand by spring, and the only person who can convert it is me, voluntarily, in writing."

"And why," Bao Wu said slowly, "would you do that."

"Because I want a repayment schedule I can survive, and because I want you off my stairs, and because there is a child in that wardrobe." Chen Zuang's voice did not change at all. "Here is the offer. I acknowledge the principal, one forty. I acknowledge interest at the ceiling. That's the number, in writing, chopped, in front of a lawyer's clerk on Monday, which makes it an asset you can sell or pledge instead of a fairy story you tell your boss. In exchange: nothing before Monday, nothing in this building ever again, and when you want me you call me and I come to you."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no," Chen Zuang agreed, "and you go back to Boss Yan and you explain why the one debtor on your book who was ready to sign a legally enforceable acknowledgment walked away from the table. And in about ten seconds you're going to remember that you've been telling Boss Yan for four months that this loan is fine."

The room was very quiet.

Bao Wu looked at the paper. Then he looked, for a long moment, at Chen Zuang's face, and something moved behind his eyes, something like a man checking a familiar road and finding a river across it.

"You don't talk like you," he said.

"No."

"Monday," Bao Wu said, and stood, and jerked his head at his friends. At the door he stopped. "Chen Zuang. That night at the parlor, you cried and you licked my shoe. You remember that?"

"I remember," Chen Zuang said. "I'd do it again if it were useful. It isn't."

When the door shut, he stood without moving until the footsteps reached the bottom of the stairs.

Then he opened the wardrobe. Nuonuo was sitting in the dark on a pile of winter clothes with both hands over her ears, and when the light came in she looked up at him with her mother's eyes and did not move.

"They're gone," he said. "You did that very well. I'm going to leave this door open, and you come out when you want to, and if you'd rather stay in there a while that's fine too. Nobody's going to make you."

He stepped back and turned around, and Shu Mang was standing exactly where she had been, with the knife still in her hand.

She had not put it down. She was not looking at the door where the men had gone. She was looking at him, and for the first time since he had woken up in this body, her face was not blank.

It was afraid in a completely new way.

Because a violent fool is a thing she had learned to survive. She had four years of instruments for it. She knew where he kept the money, she knew what set him off, she knew how long it lasted.

She had no instruments at all for a man who could sit a loan shark down in her kitchen and take him apart with a piece of paper.

"What are you," she said.

## Chapter Five: The Bag Under the Wardrobe

He had been prepared for that question since the first morning. He had drafted eleven answers and rejected all of them, and standing in that kitchen he understood that every one had been designed to make her feel better, which meant every one had been designed to make himself feel better, and that was the same disease the last owner of this body had died of.

So he told her the truth, or the part of it that was survivable.

"I don't remember most of it," he said. "The last few years. It's like hearing about a man in a story. I know what he did. I don't feel like him." He kept his hands open at his sides. "That isn't an excuse and I'm not offering it as one. He did it. This body did it. I'm not going to stand here and ask you to separate them, because you can't, and it would be obscene to ask."

"People say this," she said. "You said this. In June you said this."

"I know."

"You knelt down over there and you said it and I believed you." Her voice did not rise. That was the worst part. It stayed low and reasonable, the trained voice, the thirty-second voice. "And then it was July."

"I know."

"So don't." The knife was still in her hand. She noticed it, and set it down very deliberately on the board, and he understood that she was showing him she was not afraid to be unarmed, and that this was a lie she was telling with her body because she thought she had to. "Don't say it. Whatever it is. I'm tired."

Chen Zuang nodded.

Then he went to the wardrobe, got down on the floor, and pulled out the bag.

She made a sound then. The first uncontrolled sound he had heard from her, a small in-drawn thing, and she moved toward the door, and he stopped everything and stayed absolutely still on the floor with the bag in front of him.

"I'm not taking it," he said. "Look at my hands. I'm not touching what's inside. I want you to see me put it somewhere better."

She stood in the doorway, poised, ready, and did not run.

"It's in the wrong place," he said. "The wardrobe is on the far side of the room from the door, so if you have to go, you have to cross the room to get it, and that's four seconds you don't have. And it's a big bag. You'll leave it because it's heavy and then you'll have nothing." He set it upright. "So here's what I'd do. Small bag by the door, behind the shoe rack. Documents, both ID cards, Nuonuo's household registration copy, and cash. The big bag is a fantasy. The small bag is a plan."

He took out the household registration book and held it up so she could see it, and set it down on the floor.

"Second thing. All the paper in this house that matters is in one shoebox, and I know where it is, which means it is not safe. Tomorrow I want you to take the registration book and Nuonuo's birth certificate to someone I don't know about and leave them there. Your sister, a colleague, a locker, I don't want to be told which."

"Why are you saying this."

"Third thing." He reached into his jacket and took out an envelope with the machining shop's name printed on it and put it on the floor between them, and stepped back. "Eight hundred and sixty yuan. It's a job. Qi Debiao's shop, end of Fuxing Lane, I sweep and I run the parts to the plating shop. You can walk down there tomorrow and look through the shutter and see if I'm lying. Please do. I want you to check everything I ever tell you, for years, until you're bored of checking."

Shu Mang looked at the envelope on the floor and did not pick it up.

"And what do you want for it."

"Nothing."

"Everything has a price with you."

"Then here's the price," Chen Zuang said. "I want you to keep the bag. That's it. That's the whole price. Keep it packed, keep it by the door, and if any day, any hour, for any reason, or for no reason you can put into words, you want to take Nuonuo and go, I will not follow you, I will not call, I will not stand in the stairwell. And I'll keep sending money to wherever you tell me to, or to a lawyer if you don't want me knowing where you are."

Something crossed her face that he could not read.

"You're not sending money anywhere," she said. "You don't have any money."

"Not yet," Chen Zuang said.

He picked up his coat, because the room needed to be hers for a while, and stopped at the door.

"Shu Mang. There's a certificate at the bottom of that box. Provincial olympiad, first place." He watched her flinch, and made himself go on. "The shop's books are a disaster. Old Qi does them in a notebook with a pencil and he's out by eleven thousand over two years and doesn't know it. If you ever wanted the work, it's yours, in your name, paid to your account, and it has nothing to do with me and I'd never see the ledger."

He was in the stairwell when the door opened behind him.

"Chen Zuang."

He turned.

She was holding the envelope. Not opened. Just held, in both hands, the way you hold something you have not decided about.

"Eleven thousand out over two years," she said. "In whose favor?"

"His. He's been undercharging for plating."

Shu Mang looked at him for four full seconds. Then she shut the door, and he heard the bolt go across, and he stood in the cold stairwell and found that he was smiling like an idiot at a wall.

## Chapter Six: Ruiheng, December

He built it in the room above the workshop, in the evenings, out of scrap.

A desk from a pallet and two crates. A second-hand laptop that cost him three weeks of envelopes, bought from a repair stall and reformatted twice. A phone number registered to nothing. He worked from seven until midnight and walked home in the cold and slept four hours and did not drink, and the body screamed about that for eleven days and then, sullenly, gave up.

Qi Debiao delivered eighty thousand parts in eight lots over nine days. Fanglin Precision's purchasing office noticed on day eleven and made three phone calls, each one louder than the last, and then went quiet, exactly as Chen Zuang had said they would, because a warehouse full of signed-for stock is a different conversation from a rejection notice. They paid in March, late and partial and only after a lawyer's letter, but they paid, and by then it did not matter, because the shop had been paid for twice already by what he did in December.

Ruiheng Materials went from eleven yuan to nineteen on the lithium contract that autumn, exactly as he remembered. He put in every fen he had, which was nine thousand two hundred yuan in a nominee account, and he got out at seventeen on the eighth of December, four days before the auditors walked in and the stock went to four.

Fourteen thousand one hundred yuan.

It was, he reflected, the least money he had ever made in a single trade by a factor of about nine thousand, and he sat in the cold room above the lathes and looked at the number on the screen for a long time with his heart going like a boy's.

Because it was not fourteen thousand yuan. It was proof. It was proof that the world still worked the way he understood it to work, that the machine in his head had come across with him, and that the only thing separating him from everything he had ever had was time and a great deal of extremely boring compounding.

He wrote three numbers on a scrap of paper and pinned it above the desk where nobody but him would ever see it.

**382,000.** The debt.
**1,200,000.** A flat in a district with a decent primary school, in her name only.
**And under those, a third number with no explanation, which was what it would take to buy Fanglin Precision's parent group outright.**

He did not touch that third number for a long time. But he looked at it every night.

By February, Chunfu Trading existed on paper: a two-person trading company registered in the name of Qi Debiao's widowed sister in Wuxi, who received four thousand yuan a month for the use of her name and asked no questions at all after the first one. Chunfu owned twelve percent of a machining shop and a brokerage account and nothing else, and it had no visible director, and the man who ran it swept a floor for eight hundred and sixty yuan a week and came home smelling of cutting oil.

Shu Mang kept the bag by the door.

She also, in January, started doing the shop's books. In her own name, paid to her own account, an account he had never seen and never asked about. She came in on Sunday mornings and worked at the bench downstairs with Nuonuo drawing at her feet, and she did not speak to him more than the day required, and once, when he came down to get a part, he heard her arguing on the phone with the plating supplier about a two percent discrepancy in a way that made Qi Debiao raise both eyebrows at the ceiling and mouth: *she's terrifying.*

In February, Nuonuo spoke to him.

She had been drawing on the back of a delivery note. He had made a rule for himself that he did not approach her, ever, that she came to him or nothing happened, and for four months nothing had happened. Then a shadow arrived at his elbow while he was counting steel blanks and a small voice said:

"That's the wrong pile."

He looked down. She was pointing at the tray of rejects.

"You're right," he said, without moving, because he was afraid that if he moved she would go. "How did you know?"

"They're shiny on the end. The good ones aren't shiny."

Chen Zuang, who had once been listed at number sixty-one in the mainland and had a building with his name on it, put his hands flat on the bench and breathed carefully.

"Would you like a job?" he said. "One yuan a day. You point at the shiny ones."

Nuonuo considered this with enormous seriousness.

"Two," she said.

## Chapter Seven: The Wedding at the Xinghe Hotel

Shu Mang's cousin got married in April at the Xinghe Hotel, and the invitation came addressed to Shu Mang alone, which was itself a sentence with the verb removed.

"You don't have to go," Chen Zuang said.

"My mother will be there." She was ironing the one dress. "If I don't go she tells them I couldn't afford it, and if I go alone she tells them you've left, and either way she tells them, so." She set the iron down. "Nuonuo's coming. She likes the fish."

"Would it be easier or harder if I came?"

She looked at him in the mirror.

Six months. Six months of envelopes on Fridays, of a bag by the door that had been repacked twice and never used, of a man who slept on the floor by the door and had never once, not in any hour of any night, come near her. Six months in which she had waited, with the patient dread of an expert, for July, and July had not come.

"Harder," she said. "Come anyway."

It went the way he had known it would go from the moment he saw the seating card, which had them at table nineteen, by the service door, beside a pillar.

Her mother did not look at him. Her uncle looked at him too much. Her cousin's new husband was a sales manager at a car dealership and had the specific loudness of a man who has just moved one rung up a very short ladder, and by the third course he had made three jokes about people who play mahjong for a living, each one landing in the small silence around table nineteen like a coin in an empty bowl.

And then, over the fish, Shu Mang's mother said, to the table and not to her daughter: "She had an offer, you know. Accounting, at the university. Threw it away."

"Ma," Shu Mang said.

"I'm only saying what happened."

"Threw it away on *that*," the uncle said, and did not gesture, which was worse.

Chen Zuang set down his chopsticks.

He could end it in four sentences. He had the numbers in his head: the dealership's floor plan financing, the cousin's husband's likely commission structure, the eighty thousand her uncle had borrowed against a shop lease last spring. He could take this table apart the way he had taken Bao Wu apart, and they would remember it for twenty years.

And Shu Mang would spend the rest of the night being the woman whose husband made a scene, and the rest of her life being the subject of the story, and it would be his again, all of it, the way everything in that apartment had once been his to smash.

He picked up the teapot and refilled her mother's cup instead.

"She didn't throw it away," he said pleasantly. "It was taken. By me. I was the one who did that, and I'd be grateful if you'd say so plainly at family occasions, because she gets blamed for it and it's mine."

Silence around the table.

"She's doing the books for a machining works in Fuxing Lane," he went on, in the same tone. "Found eleven thousand yuan of underbilling in the first fortnight. If any of you needs a bookkeeper, her rate's ninety an hour and she's worth three hundred. Auntie, more tea?"

Nobody at table nineteen said anything else for a while. On the far side of the room the band started up.

Under the tablecloth, unseen by anyone, a hand closed briefly around two of his fingers, tight enough to hurt, and let go.

They walked home because the taxi was thirty yuan. Nuonuo fell asleep on his back somewhere around the second bridge, which had never happened before, and he walked the rest of the way very carefully, like a man carrying a full cup.

"Ninety an hour," Shu Mang said, at the bottom of the stairs.

"You're underpricing."

"You made that number up in front of my mother."

"I made up the ninety," Chen Zuang admitted. "The three hundred is real."

She unlocked the door. She stood in it a moment, and the light from the stairwell was behind her so he could not see her face.

"There's a quilt," she said. "In the wardrobe. The floor by the door is cold."

It was not forgiveness. He knew exactly what it was, which was a quilt, and he thought about it for most of the night, lying warm for the first time in six months, listening to his wife and his daughter breathe.

## Chapter Eight: A New Hand at the Table

In May, a man in an office on the thirty-first floor of the Fanglin Group tower in Shenzhen frowned at a screen.

Xu Jinghe was thirty-eight and ran the group's capital markets arm, and he had a habit that his subordinates found unnerving, which was that he read the whole tape rather than the summaries. He was looking at a small-cap called Weilan Optical, which Fanglin had been quietly accumulating for eleven weeks in six percent slices through four different brokers, with the care of a man moving furniture at night.

Somebody else was there.

Not big. Not loud. But the pattern was wrong. Every time Fanglin's buying paused, someone bought thirty seconds later and slightly cheaper. Every time Fanglin pushed, that someone stopped. It was not front-running, it was too polite for that. It was somebody sitting at the same table, watching his hands, and waiting for his tells with the patience of a crocodile.

"Who is this," he said.

His analyst pulled the disclosures. "A company called Chunfu Trading, registered in Wuxi. Registered capital of two hundred thousand. Legal representative is a fifty-eight-year-old woman with no other holdings. Position's small, Director Xu, it's under nine million yuan, it's noise."

"It's not noise," Xu Jinghe said. "Noise doesn't wait."

He looked at the name a while longer.

"Find out who's actually behind it."

Four hundred kilometers north, in a room above a machining workshop in Fuxing Lane, a man in a jacket that smelled of cutting oil closed a second-hand laptop and looked at the piece of paper pinned above the desk.

The first number was crossed out. Yongfa had been paid in full in March, the whole restructured principal, and Bao Wu had handed over the original loan documents with a look on his face like a man watching weather he did not understand.

The second number was not crossed out yet, but it had a date next to it now, and the date was in eighteen months, and he believed it.

The third number had no date. It sat there at the bottom of the page, enormous and patient.

Chen Zuang put on his coat, turned off the light, and went down the stairs to walk his wife and daughter home.
